Schizophrenia is a mental disorder that is usually diagnosed by a psychiatrist and affects an individual's daily life.

For some people, it is a lifelong mental disorder that causes them to behave, feel, or think differently than other people.

Schizophrenia can cause psychotic symptoms like hallucinations or delusions.

However, not all people with schizophrenia have these symptoms all the time.

Schizophrenia can also cause problems with cognition and mood.

Schizophrenia is a common disorder, affecting one in two hundred people.

While it is believed that genetics, environment, and brain chemistry play a role in such a diagnosis, it is uncertain where schizophrenia originates from.

If one child in a family shows clear signs of schizophrenia, there is a higher risk that other family members will have these same symptoms.

People with schizophrenia typically have a higher risk of self-harm compared to the general population.

This might be due to hallucinations, depression, and other factors.

Prevention is difficult because there is no reliable way to find out in advance who will get schizophrenia.

Schizophrenia is treatable.

Many people with the disorder live normal lives with a combination of therapy and medications.

No medical tests can show that a person has schizophrenia.

People are diagnosed based on the symptoms they report and show.

The symptoms of schizophrenia fall into three main categories: positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and cognitive symptoms.

Positive symptoms are thoughts, behaviors, or anything experienced by the senses that are not shared by others like hearing voices that are not really there.

These symptoms may include having strange thoughts that do not make sense, having difficulty organizing thoughts and speech, and feeling, hearing, seeing, smelling, or tasting things that do not exist.

A person is having negative symptoms when they do not have thoughts, behaviors, or emotions that healthy people have.

Negative symptoms essentially take away from life and day-to-day interactions, making it harder for people with schizophrenia to have meaningful relationships with the people around them.

Cognitive symptoms can include problems paying attention, not remembering things, not having a concept of time, and being unable to plan and organize.

Of all of schizophrenia's symptoms, cognitive symptoms are the most disabling because they affect day-to-day activities.

There are many risk factors that can increase a person's chances of developing schizophrenia.

They include trauma and genetics.

Environmental risk factors include drug use, stress before birth, being exposed to infectious diseases, feeling socially isolated, social adversity, conflict in the family, unemployment, poor housing conditions, childhood abuse, and trauma.
